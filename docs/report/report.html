Section 1

Executive summary

CloudServe Solutions has about 150 staff, and its six support agents handle more than 500 tickets a week. A customer waits 8 to 12 hours for a first reply, although the service agreement says two, and about 42% of tickets are resolved on first contact. Marcus Adeyemi, the head of support, asked for a chatbot to answer the easy tickets without adding headcount.

The ticket data pointed somewhere else. In the 500 development tickets, 71.4% are marked answerable from the existing knowledge base. The answers are there. Agents struggle to find them, and escalated tickets reach specialists with no notes on what has already been tried. A chatbot that replies on its own would also run into the one thing Marcus said would sink the project, a confident wrong answer.

So I built an assistant for the agents. It reads a ticket, labels the intent and urgency, pulls the relevant passages from the 29 support articles, and drafts a reply that cites them. Tickets that need a specialist get an escalation summary instead of a draft. An agent reads everything before it goes anywhere, and the code has no way to send a message to a customer. Every step is written to a decision log, and guardrails stop tickets and drafts that carry credentials or instruction-like text.

Urgency was right for 43.8% of tickets, which is exactly what answering "medium" every time would score, so urgency only orders the agent's queue and never changes a decision. The latency figure was taken under free-tier rate limits. The numbers here describe how well the assistant classifies, retrieves and fails safely; they are not evidence that it improves CloudServe's support results.
Section 2

The problem

2.1 What CloudServe asked for

CloudServe sells cloud infrastructure and developer tooling to just over 200 corporate customers. Tickets reach its six agents by email, chat, comments on documentation pages and a user forum, and the team is not coping. Marcus Adeyemi reports a first reply time of 8 to 12 hours against a two-hour agreement, first contact resolution of 42% against an industry figure he puts at 65%, and customer satisfaction that has dropped to 3.2 out of 5. He asked for a chatbot that handles the easy tickets so that response times fall without new hires. He was equally clear about what would make the project fail: wrong answers, more work for his agents, worse service for enterprise customers, and decisions nobody can explain.

The brief gives four more voices, in interview transcripts. Each of them sees one part of the operation, and Table 1 sets out what they said and what they could not see.

2.2 What the data showed

The documentation is not where the gap is. In the 500 development tickets, 71.4% are labelled answerable from the existing articles. More than half of all tickets (56.2%) were escalated, and 49.1% of the escalated ones were answerable from documentation, which lines up with Daniel's "about half". CloudServe holds most of the answers but cannot put them in front of the right person quickly, and it loses time a second time when a ticket does need a specialist, because the hand-over carries no context.

The gap CloudServe asked for automation. The evidence supports help with finding and drafting: locate the article, draft a cited reply, say when there is nothing to go on, and pass on what has been tried. It also says the cost of a mistake is high, since the head of support himself lists a wrong answer as a way the project fails. Both points argue for an assistant whose work an agent checks, not one that writes to customers by itself.

Three things stayed out of scope from the start. The project does not rewrite the knowledge base, because retrieval already finds the right article for nearly every answerable ticket. It does not replace any agent. And it must not widen the service gap Ravi worries about, so every result in this report is also looked at by customer group (Section 8).

Section 3

Discovery findings

The evidence came from the five transcripts and from the 500 development tickets, each of which carries labels and a record of how people handled it. Every figure below comes from those tickets. The full tables are in the Stage 1 workbook.

3.1 The answers exist

357 of the 500 tickets (71.4%) are labelled answerable from the current articles. Historically, 56.2% were escalated, and 49.1% of those escalated tickets were answerable from documentation. This is the finding that moved the design away from a model that answers from its own knowledge. The system retrieves an article and cites it. In this sample first contact resolution is 43.8% and average satisfaction 2.97 out of 5, against the 42% and 3.2 that Marcus reports. The gap could come from different periods or definitions, and I treat Marcus's figures as the operating baseline.

3.2 The minutes go to a few kinds of ticket

Resolution minutes are my measure of effort. The average ticket takes 421.7 minutes. Security incidents average 785.9 minutes and make up 5.2% of tickets but 9.7% of all minutes. Feature requests average 723.9 and compliance requests 679.3. The cheap tickets are the routine ones: rate limits average 122.2 minutes, single sign-on configuration 191.0 and onboarding 202.1.

The pattern suggested a division of labour. Routine, well-documented categories are where a draft saves an agent time. Security, compliance, feature requests and unclear tickets are where a specialist is needed and where the corpus may have no answer at all, as Ines pointed out for feature requests and new incidents. I turned that into a standing rule: those four intents always escalate, whatever the model's confidence.

3.3 First contact resolution and satisfaction move together

Marcus put first contact resolution first and Ravi put waiting first. Satisfaction averages 3.41 for tickets resolved on first contact and 2.63 for the rest. It averages 3.40 for the fastest quarter of tickets and 2.63 for the slowest. The two measures overlap, since a ticket resolved first time is also a quick one, so I kept both as goals and watch satisfaction so that it does not slip while resolution improves.

3.4 The non-fluent customers

Sofia said non-fluent customers take longer and are the least satisfied. Half of that holds up. The 120 non-fluent tickets (24.0%) have a mean resolution time of 466.1 minutes against 407.7 for fluent customers. Their satisfaction, though, is 3.04 against the 2.97 average, and their first contact resolution of 45.8% is slightly above the 43.8% overall. The cost shows up as time, not as unhappiness. I treated it as a fairness risk for retrieval, which depends on a customer's wording matching the documentation, and tested it directly (Section 8).

3.5 Enterprise customers do worst

Ravi worried about a gap between business and enterprise customers. The data shows the gap, in a direction I did not expect. Enterprise tickets have the lowest first contact resolution (37.3%, against 48.2% for business and 43.1% for standard), the highest escalation rate (62.7%) and the longest mean resolution time (483.7 minutes). Any change that does not make this worse has to be checked on enterprise tickets in particular.

3.6 What nobody mentioned

Nobody raised the handling of customer information, although every ticket carries a name and an ID. That became the privacy and auditability requirements. Nobody raised differences between channels either. When I checked later, the differences I found mostly disappeared once I accounted for how near-duplicate tickets cluster (Section 7).

Problem statement, as written at the end of discovery CloudServe's support team is struggling to keep up with more than 500 tickets a week. Customers wait 8 to 12 hours for a first reply, despite the two-hour service promise, and fewer than half of tickets are resolved on first contact. Many answers already exist in the support articles, but agents struggle to find them, and escalations often arrive without clear notes on what has already been tried. Customers wait longer, and agents repeat work.

4.1 What is deliberately out of scope

The PRD lists what it does not build and why. There is no autonomous chatbot, because the head of support's worst case is a confident wrong answer and the customer wants a person to confirm. Agents are not replaced. The knowledge base is not rewritten, since most answerable tickets already have an article that retrieval finds. Agents' personal answer files are not used as sources, because Daniel warns they may be wrong. Tiers, service agreements and staffing are CloudServe's decisions.

4.2 Assumptions written down in advance

Version 1.0 recorded three assumptions: the documentation is usable as an answer source, tickets contain enough context for a useful draft, and agents will review drafts properly. Version 1.0 also rested on three that I had not written down, namely that a confidence score can decide which tickets get an automatic answer, that urgency can be predicted from the text and used to route, and that a free model provider can run the evaluation unattended. Section 9 reports how each of the six turned out.

4.3 Success measures

The measures in the PRD fall into two groups. The offline evaluation can report intent precision and recall, retrieval hit rate, a citation proxy, latency and group gaps. First contact resolution, time to first reply and satisfaction need a supervised pilot with real outcomes, so the evaluation reports them as unavailable and never fills them from the historical baseline. That is why this report cannot say whether the assistant improves CloudServe's first contact resolution.

Section 5

Architecture and design

5.1 How a ticket moves through the system

A ticket from any of the four channels is first turned into one common format. Customer fields are held apart, and the evaluation labels and historical outcomes are kept out of everything the model sees. A language model then classifies the ticket: one of 22 intents, an urgency, a confidence for each, and for urgency a short quotation from the ticket as evidence. A fixed router decides what happens next. If the intent is a security incident, compliance request, feature request or unclear request, the ticket goes to a specialist, and the system retrieves the closest articles and prepares an escalation summary. Every other ticket gets its five closest passages retrieved, then a draft written by the model from those passages only, then guardrail checks on the ticket and the draft. What comes out is either a draft waiting for an agent, or a note to a human saying why no draft was possible. Each step adds a row to the decision log.

Retrieval covers the 29 supplied articles. Each is cut into passages of about 800 characters with a 120-character overlap, embedded with the MiniLM sentence model and kept in a persistent vector index. The drafting prompt tells the model that ticket text is untrusted data, allows it to use only the retrieved passages, and requires an inline citation such as [DOC-AUTH-004] after each claim. Agents work in a small web page where they can read a draft and its sources, edit it, approve it, reject it or escalate it, and approval runs the citation and safety checks again. A separate store holds observed outcomes and agent feedback, and a dashboard summarises decisions, guardrail activations and outcomes by customer tier and fluency, with a plain statement that it makes no causal claim. The language model was Qwen 3.8 27B served by Groq on its free tier. The classification prompt is PR-03 and the drafting prompt is PR-02.

5.3 What it does not have

There is no live intake. Tickets enter through the evaluation runner, the scripts and the tests, and nothing reads a real support inbox. The system does not measure agent time and does not detect duplicate tickets, although the data has many near-duplicates (Section 9). It depends on a single model provider, and I tested what happens when that provider fails (Section 8.5) without removing the dependency.

Section 6

Implementation

6.1 How it was built

The repository was created on 26 October 2026. The first week went on the discovery workbook, the requirements, the prompt library and the sprint plan. In the second week I built the normaliser, the evaluation harness, the classifier and router, retrieval, grounded drafting with its guardrails, the escalation summary, the agent review page, outcome capture and the dashboard. The third week is for governance, this report and the video. The code has one module per job (tickets, classification, routing, retrieve, answering, guardrails, escalation, pipeline, evaluation and the stores), so each can be tested alone. The tests run with python -m pytest tests -v; 62 of them run under Python 3.9 and pass, and two files, for the review API and the dashboard, need Python 3.11.

The evaluation harness takes an input path and an output path as arguments, because the final assessment runs it on a file I have not seen. A failing ticket is recorded as an error and the run continues. The harness writes one result row per ticket, builds a metrics report, and can resume an interrupted run by reusing the tickets already done. The resume feature turned out to be essential.

6.2 What was hard

The model provider. My first full run, on 1 November, finished 6 of 80 tickets before the free provider began answering HTTP 429, "too many requests". After moving to Groq on 2 November the first attempt reached 18 tickets. I added pacing between requests, retries that wait for the reset time the provider states, and the resumable run. The final validation run still took two sessions on two days because of the daily token allowance, so the criterion of a single unattended run is not strictly met on the free tier.

Citations. My first drafting prompt let the structured citation list and the inline identifiers disagree, which would have thrown away sound drafts. Version 2 of the prompt (3 November) requires them to match, and the stored citations are taken only from the inline identifiers, each checked against the passages retrieved for that ticket. A small probe on development tickets showed the failure that remains: a draft can cite a real document that does not back its sentence, and the check cannot see that. For that reason I call it a retrieval check.

Evaluation method. The hardest problem was not a bug. My first calibration split was by text hash, and nearly every held-out ticket had a paraphrase in the fitting set. I replaced it with a split by scenario, grouping near-duplicates by shared content words (about 79 groups in the development set). The same clustering showed that intervals computed on single tickets were far too narrow, so every interval in this report resamples whole scenarios. Separately, 67 of 97 groups of identical ticket text carry different urgency labels. I report that conflict and did not relabel, because the hidden set may conflict in the same way.

Smaller problems. The vector store printed telemetry errors and reloaded the embedding model for every ticket, which I fixed by pinning a dependency and loading once. The pipeline reported its latency as empty, so the first report had no latency figures; I changed the report to use the harness's own timing and added a test. A malformed ticket in an input file stopped the whole run, which I found while testing failure handling (Section 8.5) and fixed.

6.3 What I would change

I would add a live intake, so that the fallback which sends a ticket to review when classification fails is something a real queue uses; at the moment only the tests and a demonstration call it. I would have the pipeline record where each ticket's time goes, waiting and retrieval and each model call, which it does not do now. I would keep the prompts together with their versions in one place instead of partly inside the code. And I would build the two draft fixes described in Section 9, which need a fresh, unseen set of tickets to evaluate because the validation set has been used.

Section 7

Evaluation

7.1 Method and data

Three sets of tickets serve three purposes. The 500 development tickets were for analysis, fitting calibration and choosing between prompts and rules. The 80 validation tickets were held back for the final comparison and used for no tuning. The hidden 120-ticket set has not been given to me, so I have run the system on it zero times; the harness takes any input path and writes the same report, so the course can run it.

The validation run in this section used the final configuration: classification prompt PR-03 version 4, router version 4, drafting prompt PR-02 version 2, and confidence calibration switched off. All 80 tickets were processed with no errors. The free provider's daily allowance split the run into two resumed sessions, and it was complete by 10 November 2026. The report file was regenerated on 11 November to fill in a latency block that had come out empty.

Paraphrases of one scenario are close to identical, so tickets are not independent. Every interval here is a 95% interval from resampling whole scenarios, 2,000 times (50 scenarios in the validation set, 76 in the development predictions). Resampling single tickets would give intervals about half as wide. Where two systems are compared on the same tickets I use the paired difference.

7.2 Business outcomes

I cannot report the outcomes CloudServe cares about. First contact resolution, reply time and satisfaction need real tickets answered with and without the assistant, and the prototype answers none, so those fields in the report are empty by design. For context, the validation tickets' own history shows what human handling achieved: first contact resolution of 46.3%, escalation of 53.8%, repeat contact of 21.3%, a mean resolution time of 441 minutes and satisfaction of 2.6 out of 5. That is the baseline a pilot would have to beat. It is not a result of the system.

7.4 Changing the prompt

The first classification prompt (PR-03 version 2) reached 85.6% intent accuracy on the development predictions (interval 77.2 to 92.8). Errors were concentrated: 37 of the 49 fell in 8 of 76 scenarios. One was a compliance request, an auditor asking for access records, read as a data export, which would have been answered when it should have been escalated. I wrote version 4 of the prompt with explicit intent definitions and tie-break rules. Before running it I wrote down a hypothesis, H1: intent accuracy improves, no class loses more than 10 points, and macro recall rises.

On the development tickets accuracy rose to 93.2%, a paired gain of 7.6 points (interval 1.6 to 14.7), with 30 tickets fixed and 4 broken. The must-escalate tickets caught went from 56 of 64 to 64 of 64. H1, however, was not met, because recall for authentication failure fell by 21 points. I recorded that verdict as written and adopted the prompt anyway. The class that lost is a pair of labels that overlap and both routes end with a person, and the gains were larger. It was a decision, not a pass, and the revision log says so.

I used the validation set once, for the final prompt, and tuned nothing on it. That also means I have no unseen data left on which to test another prompt change.

7.5 Confidence, calibration and routing

I had hoped to use the model's own confidence as a gate, answering only above a calibrated 0.80. With this model that does not work. PR-03 version 4 produces only three confidence values for intent: 0.85, 0.95 and 0.98. On the development predictions, intent was right for 94.0% of the 151 tickets at 0.98 and 92.6% of the 188 at 0.95, a gap of 1.4 points. On validation the gap is wider (97.6% of 41 against 84.2% of 38) but rests on few tickets, and since every value sits above 0.80 a gate at that threshold would remove nothing either way. I fitted three isotonic calibrations, split by scenario. The latest is flat, and none could be shown to separate right answers from wrong ones. Calibration stays off, which sends every ordinary ticket to review.

That explains the routing numbers. The supplied labels expect 48 of the 80 validation tickets to be answered automatically and 32 to be escalated. The system escalated 17 and sent 63 to an agent, so strict routing accuracy is 17.5% (14 of 80). Of the 32 labelled for escalation, 14 were escalated and 18 went to review, where an agent sees them. Nothing was answered automatically. I report the figure as it is: the prototype chooses not to automate.

7.6 Retrieval and the drafts

Retrieval worked well. On development an expected article was among the five retrieved for 95.8% of answerable tickets, and was the top result for 88.8%. On validation it was among the five for 98.1%. Of the 63 tickets that reached drafting, 50 produced a cited draft and 13 returned a note that the evidence was insufficient.

Reading the drafts against the cited articles showed what the automatic checks miss. All 50 drafts passed citation validation, yet some were wrong for the ticket. VAL-0049 had the subject "Need to rotate an exposed key" over a body describing a read-only scope error; the draft told the customer to revoke the key as exposed. VAL-0078 had a subject about a slow database restore over a body about a connection-pool error, and the draft discussed restore times the customer never mentioned. VAL-0051 and VAL-0076 were Standard-plan customers told about features that the cited articles limit to Business and Enterprise plans. These examples are the basis for two changes to the requirements in Section 9.

7.7 Latency

The median was 6.3 seconds and the 95th percentile 55.8 seconds, which misses the 3-second target. Fifty-seven tickets finished in under 10 seconds and 21 took between 42 and 62 seconds. The run used a rate-limited free tier with request pacing and retries inside the measured time, so these are end-to-end times under those conditions and not a production figure. A fair measurement needs an unpaced run on a key with higher limits, which I did not make.

7.9 What I did not measure

I did not measure the hallucination rate or citation accuracy, because that needs two people to judge the drafts and I did not arrange it. I did not measure first contact resolution, reply time or satisfaction, since there was no pilot. I did not run an unpaced latency test, a load test or a cost per ticket. I did not run the hidden set. Groups of 8 to 61 tickets cannot show a gap smaller than about 20 points, so "no evidence of a gap" in Section 8 is a weaker statement than "no gap".

Section 8

Governance and risk

The governance framework offers one test: pick a way the system could harm a customer and ask what in the design stops it. Where my answer was only a property of the model, I have said so and not counted it as a control. The full governance document is docs/governance.md in the repository.

8.1 Decision logging

Every ticket writes a classification row and a routing row, plus either a drafting row or an escalation-summary row, and agent actions add a review row. The log holds every field in the framework's minimum record. On the final validation run it reconciles exactly: 80 tickets, 80 classification rows, 80 routing rows, 63 drafting rows and 17 escalation rows, so every ticket has a draft or a summary and none has both. Three weaknesses remain. The list of alternatives is never filled, because the classifier returns one label. The drafting row stores the full ticket text and passages so that the review page can show them, which means access to the database needs a retention rule that CloudServe has not set. And before my coverage check a classifier failure left no row at all; it now writes an error row.

8.4 Guardrails and the demonstration

The framework names five guardrails. The confidence floor is in place: a missing or out-of-range score goes to review. Private data and instruction integrity are partly covered, since they scan the ticket and the draft for credential-like strings and phrases such as "ignore the previous instructions" and nothing more. Grounding is partly covered: it requires an inline citation to a retrieved document and does not test the claim. Tone and scope, which should stop the system promising a refund or a deadline, is not built. When a guardrail fires, the draft is blocked, the ticket goes to review and the block is logged.

The validation run produced no block, so I tried to provoke one. Seven tickets built to make the model repeat a credential or write an injected sentence all failed, because the model refused or pointed out the credential. That is good behaviour, but it belongs to the model and not to a control I wrote, so it does not satisfy criterion A7. I added a check on the ticket itself. If the subject or body contains a credential-like or instruction-like string, the pipeline skips drafting, writes a block decision naming the category and not the secret, and routes the ticket to review. Four engineered tickets sent live through the real pipeline and model were all blocked, with a logged block and no draft. The same patterns match none of the 580 development and validation tickets, so the check would not have changed an earlier result, although the validation run predates it.

8.5 Failure handling

I induced each failure the specification names. With the provider's address pointed at a closed port, a two-ticket run finished and logged both errors; a disconnection during drafting produced a note for a human to retry later. A rejected key, a timeout and rate limiting (the last two simulated, with retries) gave the same safe result. An empty retrieval and a retrieval crash each produced a note for review. One malformed ticket in a file of three stopped the whole run, which was a defect; the harness now skips invalid records, lists them and processes the rest. When classification is unavailable, an option sends the ticket to review with no intent and writes a routing row, so the log still reconciles. Nothing in the prototype uses that option yet, since there is no live intake.

The kill switch is the weakest part of the design. There is no dedicated switch. The system cannot send to a customer, and automatic routing is off whenever the calibration setting is unset, but turning anything off means editing the environment file and restarting the service. That takes about a minute, applies from the next start and not the next ticket, and is open to anyone who can edit the file. I have not tested restart-to-stop as a switch. Before any real deployment I would add a flag read on every ticket, with a named operator.

Declaration, in the framework's words This system must never send a message to a customer without an agent approving it, or answer a security incident, compliance request, feature request or unclear request without a specialist. What enforces that: the code has no send path, approval is a separate agent action that runs the citation and safety checks again, and a routing rule escalates those four intents whatever the confidence (14 of 14 on validation, 64 of 64 on development). The most likely way it could still do harm: a fluent, cited draft that gets one claim wrong or applies an article to the wrong plan, which a busy agent approves. We would not deploy it without: a two-assessor review of at least 50 drafts, a supervised pilot, the plan-scope and commitments checks, a tested runtime kill switch, and agreed rules for data access and retention.

9.2 What it taught me

I started from the idea that confidence and urgency would work as control signals: a score would say which tickets could be answered and urgency which needed a person. The data said otherwise. What the interviews actually asked for was help finding the right article and a draft an agent can check, so the real job was making the agent's review dependable, not deciding what to automate. I would also look at the data before writing a target. Counting the repeated-text groups in the first week (67 of 97 with conflicting urgency labels) and comparing against the always-"medium" baseline would have shown that an urgency target meant nothing, and a small calibration check on 50 tickets would have done the same for confidence. I did neither until the second week.

Several things I chose not to change are in the log with the reason. I did not relabel the conflicting urgency groups, because the hidden labels may conflict in the same way. I did not switch calibration on, because the gate filtered nothing. I did not rewrite the knowledge base, because retrieval already finds an article for 98% of answerable tickets. And I kept the revised classification prompt after its hypothesis failed.

Section 10

Conclusions

CloudServe asked for a chatbot and I built an assistant for its agents. The discovery evidence favoured that: most of the answers already exist, the expensive tickets are the ones a machine should not handle alone, and the head of support named a confident wrong answer as the way the project fails. On the validation set the assistant labelled intent correctly for 91.3% of tickets, retrieved an expected article for 98.1% of the answerable ones, escalated all 14 tickets that policy sends to a specialist, kept a decision log that matched the tickets processed, and failed safely in each condition I could induce. It never sends anything to a customer.

What it achieves for CloudServe is a different question, and the evidence does not answer it. Nothing was tested with agents on real tickets, so there is no first contact resolution, reply time or satisfaction result. The hallucination rate and citation accuracy are unmeasured. Latency is well over target under free-tier limits. The hidden set has not been run. Appendix E lists these and the smaller items that are still open.

10.1 What I would do next

  1. Measure the drafts. Run the two-assessor review of 50 drafts with agreement reported. The packet, blank score sheets and scoring script are in the repository (evaluation/human_review).
  2. Fix the two draft problems. Treat a subject that conflicts with the body as unconfirmed, and compare an article's plan scope with the customer's plan. Both are written into the requirements, and checking them needs a fresh set of tickets.
  3. Close the guardrail gaps. Add the commitments check (no promises about refunds or timelines), widen the private-data patterns beyond credentials, and test paraphrased injection attempts.
  4. Build a live intake and a runtime kill switch, with a named operator and a test that it works.
  5. Run a supervised pilot with a few Tier One agents on real tickets, recording review time, edits, rejections, first contact resolution and satisfaction, split by tier and fluency. Enterprise customers already have the lowest first contact resolution in the data, so they need particular attention.
  6. Measure latency without pacing, on a key with higher limits, recording where each ticket's time goes.
