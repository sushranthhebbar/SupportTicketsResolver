Reading note. Every performance figure in this report states what it was measured on. Three data sets are used: 500 development tickets (used for analysis and for choosing prompts and rules), 80 validation tickets (used for the final comparison and not for tuning), and a hidden set of 120 tickets that I was never given. Where something was not measured, the text says so.

1. Executive summary

CloudServe Solutions is a company of about 150 people whose support team of six agents receives more than 500 tickets a week. Customers wait 8 to 12 hours for a first reply against a two-hour agreement, and about 42 percent of tickets are resolved on first contact. The head of support asked for a chatbot that answers the easy tickets so that response times improve without more staff. My discovery work suggested that this was not quite the problem. In the 500 development tickets, 71.4 percent are marked answerable from the existing knowledge base, so the answers largely exist; the difficulty is that agents struggle to find them and that escalated tickets reach specialists without the context they need. A chatbot that answers on its own would also risk the outcome the head of support named as most damaging, which is a confident wrong answer.

I therefore built a support assistant for agents, not a replacement for them. For each ticket it identifies the intent and urgency, retrieves the relevant knowledge-base passages, drafts a reply that cites its sources, and, for the tickets a specialist must handle, prepares an escalation summary. An agent reads every draft before anything could reach a customer, and the system has no code path that sends a message. Every step is written to a decision log, and guardrails block tickets and drafts that contain credential-like or instruction-like text.

The results below come from the 80-ticket validation set, which I ran once in the final configuration. The run processed all 80 tickets with no errors. Intent classification was correct for 91.3 percent of tickets (73 of 80). The expected article was among the retrieved passages for 98.1 percent of answerable tickets (52 of 53). All 14 tickets that policy says must go to a specialist (security incidents, compliance requests, feature requests and unclear requests) were escalated. The decision log reconciled exactly with the tickets processed. Latency was poor: the median was 6.3 seconds and the 95th percentile 55.8 seconds, against a target of 3 seconds, although the run used a rate-limited free model tier and those figures include waiting for the provider. Urgency was predicted correctly for only 43.8 percent of tickets, no better than always answering "medium", so I made urgency advisory: it orders the agent's queue and never changes a decision.

Two further findings shaped the design. First, the model's confidence scores could not be turned into a safe gate for automatic answering: the model reports only three distinct values, and wrong answers occur at the highest of them. The system therefore sends ordinary tickets to review rather than answering them, and the "auto-respond" route means only that a draft is ready for an agent. Second, in the development labels, 67 of 97 groups of identical ticket text carry conflicting urgency labels, which limits what any urgency measurement can show.

The most important caveat is that I did not measure the hallucination rate or citation accuracy. Both need a human to judge whether each claim is supported, and I did not carry out that review. The automatic citation check proves only that a cited document was retrieved. My own informal read of 50 validation drafts found 39 sound, 7 with minor assumptions and 4 that would mislead a reviewer who only skims, and the automatic checks caught none of the four. First contact resolution, reply time and customer satisfaction cannot be measured offline, because the prototype resolves no real tickets, and the hidden 120-ticket set has not been run by me. The figures in this report therefore describe how well the assistant classifies and retrieves, and what it does when things fail; they are not evidence that it improves CloudServe's support outcomes.

2. The problem

2.1 What was asked for

The project brief describes CloudServe Solutions as a company of about 150 people serving just over 200 corporate customers, with a support function that has broken down. Tickets arrive by email, chat, comments on documentation pages and a user forum, more than 500 a week, and are handled by six agents in two tiers. Marcus Adeyemi, the head of support, reports that the first reply takes 8 to 12 hours although the service agreement promises two, that 42 percent of tickets are resolved on first contact against an industry figure he quotes as 65 percent, and that customer satisfaction has fallen to 3.2 out of 5. He asked for a chatbot that answers the easy tickets, so that response times improve without adding staff. He also named what would make the project fail: wrong answers, extra work for agents, poorer service for enterprise customers, and decisions nobody can explain.

I also read the transcripts of four further people who see the problem from different positions. Table 1 summarises what each of the five said and what each could not see.

2.2 What discovery revealed

Each person described a different part of one situation, and the ticket data allowed me to check their claims against each other. The documentation is not the main gap: 71.4 percent of the 500 development tickets are labelled answerable from the existing articles. Yet agents do not use the articles, customers find the answer themselves only about half the time, and 56.2 percent of tickets are escalated, of which 49.1 percent were answerable from documentation. That figure is close to Daniel's "about half". The picture is therefore of a company that has most of the answers but cannot put them in front of the right person at the right moment, and that loses time again when a ticket does need a specialist, because it arrives without context.

This differs from the request in two ways. The request was for automation, and the evidence points to retrieval and preparation: finding the right article, drafting from it, and passing on what has already been tried. And the request implied that the cost of a mistake is small, when the head of support himself names a wrong answer as a way the project fails. Both observations argue for an assistant whose output is reviewed by an agent, not one that replies to customers on its own.

2.3 The gap, stated plainly

CloudServe asked for a system that answers customers. What its own data supports is a system that helps agents answer them: one that finds the article, drafts a cited reply, says when it cannot, and prepares the hand-over for the tickets that need a specialist. The distance between the two is the reason the first reply takes eight to twelve hours and the reason a confident wrong answer would be costly. It also sets the limit of what I could honestly claim. A system that only drafts for review cannot, by itself, change the first contact resolution rate, and I have no pilot data, so I do not claim that it does.

Three further points guided the scope. The project does not rewrite the knowledge base, because retrieval already finds an expected article for nearly every answerable ticket. It does not replace any agent. And it must not widen the service gap that Ravi fears, so every performance figure is examined by customer group, as Section 8 reports.

3. Discovery findings

The discovery evidence has two sources: the five interview transcripts, and the 500 development tickets, each with a label set and a history of how the ticket was handled by people. All figures in this section come from the development tickets unless stated. The full tables are in the Stage 1 workbook, which is included in the submission. I present the findings that changed the design.

3.1 The answers exist, and most escalations did not need to happen

Of the 500 development tickets, 357 (71.4 percent) are labelled answerable from the existing articles. A little over half (56.2 percent) were historically escalated, and 49.1 percent of those escalated tickets were answerable from documentation. This supports Daniel's estimate and weakens the idea that the team lacks answers. It points to the first design decision: the system should retrieve and show the article, and cite it, instead of generating answers from the model's own knowledge. The two historical outcome measures matter for context: first contact resolution is 43.8 percent in this sample, against the 42 percent Marcus quotes, and the satisfaction average is 2.97 out of 5, against the reported 3.2. The difference between those figures and the interview figures may reflect different periods or definitions, and I treat the interview figures as the operational baseline.

3.2 The time goes to a small number of kinds of ticket

I used resolution minutes as a measure of effort. The average ticket takes 421.7 minutes. The tickets that cost the most are the ones a machine should least be trusted with: security incidents average 785.9 minutes and take 9.7 percent of all minutes from 5.2 percent of tickets; feature requests average 723.9 minutes; compliance requests average 679.3 minutes. The cheap tickets are the routine ones: rate limits average 122.2 minutes, single sign-on configuration 191.0 and onboarding 202.1. Figure 1 shows the contrast.

Two design consequences follow. Easy, well-documented categories are the right place for a draft to save time, and the hard categories (security, compliance, feature requests, and tickets that are unclear) should go to a specialist with a good summary. Because these four intents also have no guaranteed answer in the corpus, which Ines noted for feature requests and novel incidents, I made them a standing rule: they always escalate, whatever the model's confidence. Later evidence (Sections 7 and 9) is the reason this rule, not a confidence threshold, became the main safeguard.

3.3 First contact resolution matters more than speed alone

Marcus said first contact resolution matters more to him than reply time, and Ravi said waiting is the main problem. The data relates to both. Average satisfaction is 3.41 out of 5 for tickets resolved at first contact and 2.63 for tickets that were not; it is 3.40 for the fastest quarter of tickets and 2.63 for the slowest quarter. The two are not independent, because a ticket resolved first time is also fast. I concluded that the system should aim at both outcomes, and that satisfaction should be watched so that it does not fall as first contact resolution improves.

3.4 The non-fluent customer hypothesis

Sofia said non-fluent customers take longer and are the least satisfied. The data partly supports her. The 120 non-fluent tickets (24.0 percent) have a mean resolution time of 466.1 minutes against 407.7 for fluent customers. But their satisfaction is 3.04 out of 5, slightly above the 2.97 average, and their first contact resolution of 45.8 percent is slightly above the 43.8 percent overall. So the cost of non-fluency shows up as time, not as unhappiness. I treated it as a fairness risk for the retrieval step, which depends on the customer's phrasing matching the documentation, and tested it explicitly (Section 8).

3.5 Differences by tier and a surprise in the data

Ravi worried about a gap between business and enterprise customers. The data shows enterprise customers with the lowest first contact resolution (37.3 percent against 48.2 for business and 43.1 for standard), the highest escalation rate (62.7 percent) and the longest mean resolution time (483.7 minutes). I did not expect that, and it means that a system which does not widen the gap must be checked for enterprise tickets in particular. The repeat contact rate of 21.6 percent also seemed high to me, and I record it as a measure I could not influence offline.

3.6 What nobody mentioned

No one raised how customer information should be protected when tickets are processed, although every ticket carries a customer name and identifier. That led to the privacy requirement and to the rule that the log, and later the guardrails, must handle credentials and personal data. No one raised differences between channels either; I checked these later and found they largely disappeared once I allowed for the way scenarios cluster (Section 7).

3.7 The problem statement

The statement I wrote at the end of discovery reads: CloudServe's support team is struggling to keep up with more than 500 tickets a week. Customers wait 8 to 12 hours for a first reply, despite the two-hour service promise, and fewer than half of tickets are resolved on first contact. Many answers already exist in the support articles, but agents struggle to find them, and escalations often arrive without clear notes on what has already been tried. Customers wait longer, and agents repeat work.

Some things are deliberately out of scope, and the PRD says why. The system will not send replies to customers on its own, because the head of support's worst case is a confident wrong answer and the customer wants human confirmation. It will not replace agents or rewrite the knowledge base, because most answerable tickets already have an article that retrieval finds. It will not use agents' personal answer files as sources, because Daniel warns they may be wrong. And it will not change tiers, service agreements or staffing levels, which are decisions for CloudServe.

Three of the requirements rest on assumptions that I wrote down in version 1.0 so that the revision would be a considered update. The assumptions were that the documentation is usable as an answer source, that ticket records contain enough context for a useful draft, and that agents will review and edit drafts properly. Section 9 reports which held. Version 1.0 also contained three assumptions I had not written down and which turned out to matter more: that a confidence score can decide which tickets get an automatic answer, that urgency can be predicted from the text and used to route, and that a free model provider can run the whole evaluation unattended. All three were wrong.

The success measures in the PRD are split into those an offline evaluation can report (intent precision and recall, retrieval hit rate, citation proxy, latency and group gaps) and those that need a supervised pilot with real outcomes (first contact resolution, time to first reply, satisfaction). The second group is reported as unavailable, never inferred from the historical baseline. That split is the reason this report cannot say whether the assistant improves CloudServe's first contact resolution.

5. Architecture and design

5.1 The system

Figure 2 shows the path of a ticket. The assistant is a Python service with a command-line evaluation runner and a small web page for agents. A ticket from any of the four channels is first normalised into one format, with the customer fields held separately and the evaluation labels and historical outcomes kept out of anything the model sees. A language model then classifies it, returning an intent from the 22 categories in the data, an urgency, a confidence for each, and for the urgency a short quotation from the ticket as evidence. The router applies a fixed policy to the result. Tickets whose intent is security incident, compliance request, feature request or unclear request go to a specialist; the system retrieves the closest articles and prepares an escalation summary for them. For every other ticket the system retrieves passages, asks the model to draft a reply using only those passages, and runs guardrails over the ticket and over the draft. A ticket that passes becomes a draft waiting for an agent; one that fails, or has too little evidence, becomes a note to a human saying why. Every step writes a row to the decision log.

Retrieval uses the 29 supplied knowledge-base articles. Each is split into passages of about 800 characters with a 120-character overlap, embedded with the MiniLM sentence model and stored in a persistent vector index, and the five nearest passages are returned with their document identifiers. The drafting model sees only those passages and the ticket, is told that ticket text is untrusted data, and must place an inline citation such as [DOC-AUTH-004] after each claim. The agent's review page lists the drafts, shows each with its sources, and lets the agent edit, approve, reject or escalate; approving re-runs the citation and safety checks. Observed outcomes and agent feedback have their own store, and a dashboard summarises decisions, guardrail activations and outcomes by customer tier and fluency, with a plain statement that it makes no causal claim. The classifier and the drafter call an OpenAI-compatible chat endpoint, and the evaluation used the free tier of Groq running the Qwen 3.8 27-billion-parameter model. Prompts are versioned PR-02 for drafting and PR-03 for classification.

5.3 What the architecture does not do

It does not have a live intake: tickets enter through the evaluation runner, the scripts or the tests, and nothing accepts them from a real support inbox. It does not measure the time an agent spends. It does not rank or detect duplicate tickets, although I found that repeated texts are common (Section 9). And its single model provider is a point of failure whose effects I tested rather than eliminated (Section 8).

6. Implementation

6.1 How it was built

I built the system in the order the sprint plan set out. The first week produced the discovery workbook, the requirements document, the prompt library and the sprint plan, and the repository was created on 26 October 2026. In the second week I built normalisation, the evaluation harness, the classifier and router, retrieval, grounded drafting with guardrails, the escalation summary, the agent review page, outcome capture and the dashboard. The third week, which is still in progress as I write, covers the governance work, this report and the video. The code is organised in one module per concern (tickets, classification, routing, retrieve, answering, guardrails, escalation, pipeline, evaluation, the stores, and the application) so that each step can be tested without the others. The suite is run with one command, python -m pytest tests -v. On 11 November the 62 tests that run under Python 3.9 passed; the review API and dashboard tests need Python 3.11 and are run separately in that environment.

The evaluation harness takes an input path and an output path as arguments, not a hard-coded file name, because it will be run against a data set I have not seen. It records an error against any ticket that fails and carries on, writes one result row per ticket, and produces a metrics report. It can resume an interrupted run, reusing the tickets already processed without error, which turned out to be essential.

6.2 What was difficult

The provider. The first full run on 1 November completed 6 of 80 tickets before the free provider answered with HTTP 429 (too many requests). After I switched to Groq on 2 November, the first attempt reached 18 tickets, and I added pacing between requests, retries that honour the provider's stated reset time, and the resumable run. The final validation run took two sessions across two days because of the daily token allowance. This means that criterion A9, a single unattended run over the whole set, is not strictly met on the free tier. I report that in Section 7 and do not describe the run as a single unattended pass.

Citations. My first drafting prompt allowed the structured citation list and the inline identifiers in the text to disagree, which would have discarded sound drafts. The second version of the drafting prompt (PR-02 v2, 3 November) requires them to match, and the stored citations are now taken only from the inline identifiers that were checked against the passages retrieved for that ticket. A small probe on development tickets showed the failure mode, which is when the model cites a real document that does not support its sentence; the automatic check cannot catch that, which is why I treat it as a retrieval check only.

Evaluation hygiene. The hardest part was not a bug but a method problem. My first calibration split was by text hash, and I found that nearly every held-out ticket had a paraphrase in the fitting set. I replaced it with a split by scenario, where near-duplicates are grouped by shared content words (about 79 groups in the development set). I then discovered that intervals based on single tickets were too narrow for the same reason, so I computed every interval by resampling whole scenarios. Separately, 67 of 97 groups of identical ticket text carry different urgency labels; I report the conflict and did not relabel, because the hidden labels may conflict in the same way.

Smaller problems. The vector store printed telemetry errors and reloaded the embedding model for every ticket, which I fixed by pinning a dependency and loading once. A malformed ticket in an input file stopped the whole run; I found this while testing failure handling and fixed it (Section 8). And the pipeline reported its latency as empty, so the first report had no latency figures; I fixed the report to use the harness measurement and added a test.

6.3 What I would restructure

Four changes would make the next version better. First, a live intake: today the "degrade to review when classification fails" behaviour exists but nothing calls it outside the tests and the demonstration. Second, timing: the pipeline should record where each ticket's seconds went (waiting, retrieval, each model call), because I can infer from the two clusters in Figure 4 that the slow tickets were waiting for the provider but I did not log the cause. Third, the model prompts should live in one place with their versions and not partly inside the code, which would have made the prompt register in Appendix A simpler to keep honest. And fourth, the drafter should be told to treat a subject line that conflicts with the ticket body as unconfirmed, and the guardrails should compare an article's plan scope with the customer's plan; both are specified in PRD 2.0 but not built, because I found them after the validation set was spent and a change would have broken the comparison.

7. Evaluation

7.1 Method and data

I used three sets of tickets for different purposes. The 500 development tickets were used for analysis, for fitting calibration and for choosing between prompts and rules. The 80 validation tickets were kept for the final comparison, and I used them for no tuning. The hidden set of 120 tickets that the course will use has not been given to me, and I have run the system on it zero times; the harness takes any input path and writes the same report, so the course can run it. The validation run reported in this section used the final configuration: prompt PR-03 version 4 for classification, router version 4, and drafting prompt PR-02 version 2, with confidence calibration switched off. It processed all 80 tickets with no errors. Because of the provider's daily allowance it ran in two resumed sessions, finishing by 10 November 2026, and the report file was regenerated on 11 November to correct a latency block that had been empty.

Paraphrases of one scenario are nearly identical, so single tickets are not independent. Wherever I give an interval, it is a 95 percent interval from resampling whole scenarios (50 groups in the validation set, 76 in the development predictions), 2,000 times. A naive per-ticket interval would have been about half as wide. Where I compare two systems on the same tickets I use the paired difference.

7.2 Business outcomes

I cannot report the business outcomes the client cares about. First contact resolution, time to first reply and customer satisfaction need real tickets answered with and without the assistant, and the prototype answers none. The evaluation report therefore leaves those fields empty by design. For context, the validation tickets' own history shows what human handling achieved: first contact resolution of 46.3 percent, an escalation rate of 53.8 percent, a repeat contact rate of 21.3 percent, a mean resolution time of 441 minutes and a satisfaction average of 2.6 out of 5. These are the baseline a pilot would have to beat, not results of the system. The only business-relevant evidence I have is indirect: the system retrieves an expected article for nearly every answerable ticket, and prepares a summary for every escalation, which are the two things the interviews said would save agents' time. I did not measure the time saved.

7.4 Changing the prompt: a result that was not clean

The first classification prompt (PR-03 v2) reached 85.6 percent intent accuracy on the development predictions (interval 77.2 to 92.8). The error analysis showed that errors were concentrated: 37 of 49 errors fell in 8 of 76 scenarios, among them the compliance request "an auditor asked for access records" misread as a data export, which would have been answered instead of escalated. I wrote a fourth version of the prompt (PR-03 v4) with explicit intent definitions and tie-break rules, and, before running it, a pre-registered hypothesis, H1: that intent accuracy would improve with no class losing more than 10 points and macro recall rising. On the development tickets the result was 93.2 percent, a paired gain of 7.6 points (interval 1.6 to 14.7), with 30 tickets fixed and 4 broken. Must-escalate tickets caught rose from 56 of 64 to 64 of 64. But H1 was not met: recall for authentication failure fell by 21 points. I recorded that verdict as written and adopted the prompt anyway, because the lost class is a pair of labels that overlap and both go to a person, and the gains were larger. That was a decision, not a pass, and I list it as such in the revision log.

On validation the two prompts differ by two tickets, which I regard as no evidence either way. I used the validation set once for the final prompt and have not used it to tune anything. Because of that, I cannot test any further prompt change without a fresh, unseen set, which is a limitation of having only 80 validation tickets.

7.5 Confidence, calibration and routing

The model reports its own confidence and I hoped to turn it into a gate: answer only when calibrated confidence is above 0.80. It cannot be done with this model. PR-03 v4 emits only three distinct intent confidences (0.85, 0.95 and 0.98). On the development predictions, intent was right for 94.0 percent of the 151 tickets at 0.98 and 92.6 percent of the 188 at 0.95, a difference of 1.4 points. On validation the gap is larger (97.6 percent of 41 against 84.2 percent of 38) but rests on few tickets, and with a threshold of 0.80, below all three values, a gate would filter nothing either way. I fitted isotonic calibrations (three versions, split by scenario); the latest is flat, and none could be shown to separate right answers from wrong ones. I left calibration switched off, so with the artifact unset every ordinary ticket goes to review. That is the safest behaviour available, and it explains the routing result: the supplied labels expect 48 of the 80 validation tickets to be answered automatically and 32 to be escalated, whereas the system escalated 17 and sent 63 to an agent. Strict routing accuracy is therefore 17.5 percent (14 of 80), because the system never takes the automatic route. Of the 32 labelled for escalation, 14 were escalated and 18 went to review, where an agent sees them; none was answered automatically. I report routing accuracy and do not defend it as a success: it shows that the prototype deliberately declines to automate.

7.6 Retrieval and the drafts

Retrieval worked well. On the development tickets an expected article was among the five retrieved for 95.8 percent of answerable tickets (the first result for 88.8 percent), and on validation for 98.1 percent. Of the 63 tickets that reached drafting, 50 produced a draft with citations and 13 returned a note that evidence was insufficient; nine of those 13 were refusals because the model's text had no inline citation, a safe failure, although six of the nine concerned answerable tickets that a retry might have drafted.

Because I did not run the human review, I read the 50 drafts myself against the cited articles. I did not use the labels to judge them. The result is an informal check, not a rate: 39 drafts were sound, 7 contained a small assumption or embellishment, and 4 would mislead a reviewer who only skims (for example, a draft that told a customer to revoke a key as exposed when the ticket body described a permission-scope error, because the subject line said "exposed key"). Two drafts also described features restricted to plans the customer did not have. All 50 passed the automatic citation validation, which confirms that it checks retrieval and not whether a claim fits the ticket. The patterns, a subject line taken as fact, an article applied to a case or a plan it does not fit, and an unparsed multi-document citation, are the basis for two requirement changes in Section 9.

7.7 Latency

The median was 6.3 seconds and the 95th percentile 55.8 seconds, so the target of 3 seconds is not met. Figure 4 shows two clusters: 57 tickets under 10 seconds and 21 tickets between 42 and 62 seconds. Because the slow tickets appear in every group, including the escalations that make a single call, I infer that they waited for the provider's rate limit, but I did not log the cause, so this is an inference. The run also deliberately waited about 2.5 seconds before each model call. These are therefore end-to-end times under free-tier pacing, not a production figure, and a fair measurement needs an unpaced run on a key with higher limits, which I did not make.

8. Governance and risk

The Governance Framework's test is simple: pick a plausible way the system could harm a customer and ask what in the design stops it. Where my answer is a property of the model, I say so and do not count it as a control. This section covers decision logging, the risk register, the fairness audit, the guardrails, and the incident procedure. The full governance document is in the repository (docs/governance.md).

8.1 Decision logging

Every ticket writes a classification row and a routing row, plus either an answer-drafting row or an escalation-summary row; agent actions write a review row. All fields in the framework's minimum record exist: the identifier, time, stage, model and version, prediction and confidence, sources used, threshold applied, action, reason, guardrail results, prompt version and requirement identifiers. On the final validation run the log reconciles exactly: 80 tickets, 80 classification rows, 80 routing rows, 63 drafting rows and 17 escalation rows, each ticket with a draft or a summary and never both. Three weaknesses remain. The list of alternatives is never filled, because the classifier returns one label. The drafting row stores the full ticket text and the retrieved passages so that the review page can show them, so access to the database needs a retention rule that CloudServe has not yet agreed. And until the coverage check I did here, a classifier failure produced no row at all; it now writes an error row, and an option routes such a ticket to human review.

8.4 Guardrails and the demonstration

The framework lists five guardrails. The confidence floor is met: a missing or out-of-range score routes to review. Private data and instruction integrity are partial: they scan the ticket and the draft for credential-like strings and for phrases such as "ignore the previous instructions", and nothing else. Grounding is partial: it requires an inline citation to a retrieved document and does not check the claim. Tone and scope, which should stop the system promising a refund or a deadline, is missing. When a guardrail fires, the draft is blocked, the ticket goes to review, and the block is logged.

The validation run produced no block, so I tried to provoke one. Seven tickets designed to make the model repeat a credential or write an injected sentence all failed: the model refused or flagged the credential. That is good behaviour, but it is a property of the model and not a control I built, so it does not satisfy criterion A7. I therefore added a deterministic check on the ticket itself. When the subject or body contains a credential-like or instruction-like string, the pipeline skips drafting, writes a block decision with the matched category (and not the secret, which is also withheld from the log), and routes the ticket to review. Four engineered tickets submitted live to the real pipeline and model were all blocked, with a logged block and no draft. The same patterns match none of the 580 development and validation tickets, so the check would not have changed any earlier result, but the validation run predates it.

8.5 Failure handling

I induced each failure condition. With the provider address set to a closed port, a two-ticket evaluation run finished, recorded both errors and logged them; a disconnection at the drafting stage returned a note for a human with the instruction to retry later. A wrong key, a timeout (simulated), and rate limiting (simulated, with retries) produced the same safe result. An empty retrieval and a retrieval crash produced a note for review. One malformed ticket in an input file of three stopped the whole run, which was a defect; the harness now skips and lists invalid records and processes the rest. When classification is unavailable, an option routes the ticket to review with no intent and writes a routing row, so the log still reconciles; nothing in the prototype calls it, because there is no live intake.

8.6 Incident procedure and kill switch

The incident procedure has six steps. Detect: an agent reports a wrong or unsafe draft, the dashboard shows a rise in blocked or insufficient-evidence outcomes, or the decision log fails to reconcile. Contain: unset the calibration setting if it is set, so every ticket goes to review, and stop the service; do not delete any rows. Assess: query the decision log by ticket for the classification, drafting and routing rows with the prompt version, the sources and the guardrail results, and decide whether the cause was the model, retrieval, a stale article, a guardrail gap or tampering, then count similar tickets. Notify: tell the head of support and the affected agents the same day; if a customer was told something wrong, the agent corrects it through the normal channel. Remediate: fix the cause, add a test that reproduces the ticket, run the suite, then restore. Review: record what happened, update the register, and check whether the requirements need revising.

The kill switch is the weakest part. There is no dedicated switch. The system has no path that sends to a customer, and automatic routing is off whenever the calibration setting is unset, but turning anything off means editing the environment file and restarting the service, which takes about a minute, takes effect on the next start and not on the next ticket, and is open to whoever has access to that file. I have not tested restart-to-stop as a switch. A flag read on every ticket, with a named operator, is the first thing I would add before any real deployment.

8.7 Declaration

This system must never send a message to a customer without an agent approving it, nor answer a security incident, compliance request, feature request or unclear request without a specialist. The code enforces the first by having no send path, with approval a separate agent action that re-runs the citation and safety checks, and the second by a routing rule that escalates those four intents whatever the confidence (14 of 14 caught on validation, 64 of 64 on development). The most likely way it could still cause harm is a fluent, cited draft that gets one claim wrong or applies an article to a plan it does not cover, which a busy agent approves; in my read of 50 drafts four would mislead a reviewer who skims and the automatic checks caught none. I would not deploy it without a human review of at least 50 drafts by two assessors with their agreement reported, a supervised pilot with real agents, the plan-scope and commitments checks, a runtime kill switch that has been tested, and agreed rules for data access and retention.

9.1 Which assumptions failed

Of the three assumptions I wrote down, the first held largely: the documentation is usable, with an expected article retrieved for 52 of 53 answerable validation tickets, but the weakness was applicability, not coverage. The second held only in part: ticket bodies are usually enough, but subjects often disagree with them and the drafter followed the subject in six of 50 drafts. The third, that agents will review drafts properly, is untested because there was no pilot. The three assumptions I had not written down all failed. A numeric confidence could not gate automatic answers: on the development tickets the mean confidence was 0.958 when the intent was right and 0.934 when wrong. Urgency could not be predicted from the text: exact accuracy was 47.5 percent on development and 43.8 percent on validation. And a free provider could not run the whole evaluation in one unattended pass.

9.2 What the revision taught me

I assumed that confidence and urgency would work as control signals, with a score saying which tickets could be answered and urgency saying which needed a person. The data showed the opposite. What the interviews asked for was help finding the right article and a draft an agent can check, so the real problem was making the agent's review reliable, not deciding which tickets to automate. I also learned how much of an evaluation depends on how the data is split. Counting the repeated-text groups in the first week, where 67 of 97 groups had conflicting urgency labels, and checking the always-medium baseline, would have shown that an urgency target was not meaningful; and a small calibration check on 50 tickets would have shown the same about confidence. I did not do either until the second week.

I decided not to make some changes. I did not relabel the conflicting urgency groups, because the hidden labels may conflict in the same way and a system tuned to cleaned labels would not match them. I did not activate calibration, because the gate filtered nothing. I did not rewrite the knowledge base, because retrieval already finds an article for 98 percent of answerable tickets and the issue is a scope check. I kept the revised classification prompt although its pre-registered hypothesis failed, and I recorded the verdict as written. Each of these is in the "not changed" table of the revision log with the reason and the condition that would reopen it.

10. Conclusions

I built an assistant that helps CloudServe's agents, not a chatbot that replaces them, and the evidence I gathered supports that choice more than it supports the original request. On the validation set the assistant classified intent correctly for 91.3 percent of tickets, retrieved an expected article for 98.1 percent of answerable ones, escalated every ticket that policy says must go to a specialist, kept a decision log that reconciled exactly, and degraded safely in every failure condition I could induce. It never sends anything to a customer, and I think that is the right design for a team whose head of support named a confident wrong answer as a reason the project could fail.

I am less certain about what it achieves. I have no evidence that it improves first contact resolution, reply time or satisfaction, because nothing was tested with agents on real tickets. I have no measured hallucination rate or citation accuracy, so I cannot say it meets the 5 percent and 95 percent targets, and the one informal read I made suggests that roughly one draft in twelve would mislead a reviewer who does not check carefully, and that the automatic checks cannot see which. Its latency is far above the 3-second target under free-tier pacing, and I do not know what it would be on a paid key. The hidden evaluation set has not been run. These gaps are the content of the next step.

10.1 What I would do next

  1. Measure the drafts. Run the two-assessor review of the 50 drafts with agreement reported. The packet, blank score sheets and scoring script are in the repository. This would turn two empty targets into measurements.
  2. Fix the two draft problems I found. Treat a subject that conflicts with the body as unconfirmed, and compare an article's plan scope with the customer's plan. Both are specified in the requirements, and the fix needs a new, unseen evaluation set, since the validation set has been used.
  3. Close the guardrail gaps. Add the commitments check (no promises about refunds or timelines), widen the private-data patterns beyond credentials, and test paraphrased injection attempts.
  4. Build a live intake and a runtime kill switch. A flag read on every ticket, a named operator, and a test that it works.
  5. Run a supervised pilot. With a handful of Tier One agents and real tickets, measuring review time, edits, rejections, first contact resolution and satisfaction, and comparing by tier and fluency, because enterprise customers already have the lowest first contact resolution in the data.
  6. Measure latency fairly. An unpaced run on a key with higher limits, with the causes of delay logged per ticket.

10.2 What remains uncertain

Whether the prompt gain holds beyond the roughly nine scenarios that produced it. Whether agents catch the misleading drafts. Whether the fairness result for non-fluent customers survives a larger sample; with 19 tickets it could hide a gap of about 20 points. Whether the urgency labels reflect customer circumstances that are not in the ticket text, which would explain why identical texts carry different labels. And whether the free-tier model I used is representative of the model CloudServe would run. I would want to resolve these with a pilot and an unpaced evaluation before recommending that the assistant touch a real queue.
