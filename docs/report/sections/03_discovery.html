3. Discovery findings

The discovery evidence has two sources: the five interview transcripts, and the 500 development tickets, each with a label set and a history of how the ticket was handled by people. All figures in this section come from the development tickets unless stated. The full tables are in the Stage 1 workbook, which is included in the submission. I present the findings that changed the design.

3.1 The answers exist, and most escalations did not need to happen

Of the 500 development tickets, 357 (71.4 percent) are labelled answerable from the existing articles. A little over half (56.2 percent) were historically escalated, and 49.1 percent of those escalated tickets were answerable from documentation. This supports Daniel's estimate and weakens the idea that the team lacks answers. It points to the first design decision: the system should retrieve and show the article, and cite it, instead of generating answers from the model's own knowledge. The two historical outcome measures matter for context: first contact resolution is 43.8 percent in this sample, against the 42 percent Marcus quotes, and the satisfaction average is 2.97 out of 5, against the reported 3.2. The difference between those figures and the interview figures may reflect different periods or definitions, and I treat the interview figures as the operational baseline.

3.2 The time goes to a small number of kinds of ticket

I used resolution minutes as a measure of effort. The average ticket takes 421.7 minutes. The tickets that cost the most are the ones a machine should least be trusted with: security incidents average 785.9 minutes and take 9.7 percent of all minutes from 5.2 percent of tickets; feature requests average 723.9 minutes; compliance requests average 679.3 minutes. The cheap tickets are the routine ones: rate limits average 122.2 minutes, single sign-on configuration 191.0 and onboarding 202.1. Figure 1 shows the contrast.

Two design consequences follow. Easy, well-documented categories are the right place for a draft to save time, and the hard categories (security, compliance, feature requests, and tickets that are unclear) should go to a specialist with a good summary. Because these four intents also have no guaranteed answer in the corpus, which Ines noted for feature requests and novel incidents, I made them a standing rule: they always escalate, whatever the model's confidence. Later evidence (Sections 7 and 9) is the reason this rule, not a confidence threshold, became the main safeguard.

3.3 First contact resolution matters more than speed alone

Marcus said first contact resolution matters more to him than reply time, and Ravi said waiting is the main problem. The data relates to both. Average satisfaction is 3.41 out of 5 for tickets resolved at first contact and 2.63 for tickets that were not; it is 3.40 for the fastest quarter of tickets and 2.63 for the slowest quarter. The two are not independent, because a ticket resolved first time is also fast. I concluded that the system should aim at both outcomes, and that satisfaction should be watched so that it does not fall as first contact resolution improves.

3.4 The non-fluent customer hypothesis

Sofia said non-fluent customers take longer and are the least satisfied. The data partly supports her. The 120 non-fluent tickets (24.0 percent) have a mean resolution time of 466.1 minutes against 407.7 for fluent customers. But their satisfaction is 3.04 out of 5, slightly above the 2.97 average, and their first contact resolution of 45.8 percent is slightly above the 43.8 percent overall. So the cost of non-fluency shows up as time, not as unhappiness. I treated it as a fairness risk for the retrieval step, which depends on the customer's phrasing matching the documentation, and tested it explicitly (Section 8).

3.5 Differences by tier and a surprise in the data

Ravi worried about a gap between business and enterprise customers. The data shows enterprise customers with the lowest first contact resolution (37.3 percent against 48.2 for business and 43.1 for standard), the highest escalation rate (62.7 percent) and the longest mean resolution time (483.7 minutes). I did not expect that, and it means that a system which does not widen the gap must be checked for enterprise tickets in particular. The repeat contact rate of 21.6 percent also seemed high to me, and I record it as a measure I could not influence offline.

3.6 What nobody mentioned

No one raised how customer information should be protected when tickets are processed, although every ticket carries a customer name and identifier. That led to the privacy requirement and to the rule that the log, and later the guardrails, must handle credentials and personal data. No one raised differences between channels either; I checked these later and found they largely disappeared once I allowed for the way scenarios cluster (Section 7).

3.7 The problem statement

The statement I wrote at the end of discovery reads: CloudServe's support team is struggling to keep up with more than 500 tickets a week. Customers wait 8 to 12 hours for a first reply, despite the two-hour service promise, and fewer than half of tickets are resolved on first contact. Many answers already exist in the support articles, but agents struggle to find them, and escalations often arrive without clear notes on what has already been tried. Customers wait longer, and agents repeat work.
