Section 7

Evaluation

7.1 Method and data

Three sets of tickets serve three purposes. The 500 development tickets were for analysis, fitting calibration and choosing between prompts and rules. The 80 validation tickets were held back for the final comparison and used for no tuning. The hidden 120-ticket set has not been given to me, so I have run the system on it zero times; the harness takes any input path and writes the same report, so the course can run it.

The validation run in this section used the final configuration: classification prompt PR-03 version 4, router version 4, drafting prompt PR-02 version 2, and confidence calibration switched off. All 80 tickets were processed with no errors. The free provider's daily allowance split the run into two resumed sessions, and it was complete by 10 November 2026. The report file was regenerated on 11 November to fill in a latency block that had come out empty.

Paraphrases of one scenario are close to identical, so tickets are not independent. Every interval here is a 95% interval from resampling whole scenarios, 2,000 times (50 scenarios in the validation set, 76 in the development predictions). Resampling single tickets would give intervals about half as wide. Where two systems are compared on the same tickets I use the paired difference.

7.2 Business outcomes

I cannot report the outcomes CloudServe cares about. First contact resolution, reply time and satisfaction need real tickets answered with and without the assistant, and the prototype answers none, so those fields in the report are empty by design. For context, the validation tickets' own history shows what human handling achieved: first contact resolution of 46.3%, escalation of 53.8%, repeat contact of 21.3%, a mean resolution time of 441 minutes and satisfaction of 2.6 out of 5. That is the baseline a pilot would have to beat. It is not a result of the system.

7.4 Changing the prompt

The first classification prompt (PR-03 version 2) reached 85.6% intent accuracy on the development predictions (interval 77.2 to 92.8). Errors were concentrated: 37 of the 49 fell in 8 of 76 scenarios. One was a compliance request, an auditor asking for access records, read as a data export, which would have been answered when it should have been escalated. I wrote version 4 of the prompt with explicit intent definitions and tie-break rules. Before running it I wrote down a hypothesis, H1: intent accuracy improves, no class loses more than 10 points, and macro recall rises.

On the development tickets accuracy rose to 93.2%, a paired gain of 7.6 points (interval 1.6 to 14.7), with 30 tickets fixed and 4 broken. The must-escalate tickets caught went from 56 of 64 to 64 of 64. Figure 3 compares the two prompts on both sets. H1, however, was not met, because recall for authentication failure fell by 21 points. I recorded that verdict as written and adopted the prompt anyway. The class that lost is a pair of labels that overlap and both routes end with a person, and the gains were larger. It was a decision, not a pass, and the revision log says so.

I used the validation set once, for the final prompt, and tuned nothing on it. That also means I have no unseen data left on which to test another prompt change.

7.5 Confidence, calibration and routing

I had hoped to use the model's own confidence as a gate, answering only above a calibrated 0.80. With this model that does not work. PR-03 version 4 produces only three confidence values for intent: 0.85, 0.95 and 0.98. On the development predictions, intent was right for 94.0% of the 151 tickets at 0.98 and 92.6% of the 188 at 0.95, a gap of 1.4 points. On validation the gap is wider (97.6% of 41 against 84.2% of 38) but rests on few tickets, and since every value sits above 0.80 a gate at that threshold would remove nothing either way. I fitted three isotonic calibrations, split by scenario. The latest is flat, and none could be shown to separate right answers from wrong ones. Calibration stays off, which sends every ordinary ticket to review.

That explains the routing numbers. The supplied labels expect 48 of the 80 validation tickets to be answered automatically and 32 to be escalated. The system escalated 17 and sent 63 to an agent, so strict routing accuracy is 17.5% (14 of 80; Table B2 in Appendix B). Of the 32 labelled for escalation, 14 were escalated and 18 went to review, where an agent sees them. Nothing was answered automatically. I report the figure as it is: the prototype chooses not to automate.

7.6 Retrieval and the drafts

Retrieval worked well. On development an expected article was among the five retrieved for 95.8% of answerable tickets, and was the top result for 88.8%. On validation it was among the five for 98.1%. Of the 63 tickets that reached drafting, 50 produced a cited draft and 13 returned a note that the evidence was insufficient (Table B3).

Reading the drafts against the cited articles showed what the automatic checks miss. All 50 drafts passed citation validation, yet some were wrong for the ticket. VAL-0049 had the subject "Need to rotate an exposed key" over a body describing a read-only scope error; the draft told the customer to revoke the key as exposed. VAL-0078 had a subject about a slow database restore over a body about a connection-pool error, and the draft discussed restore times the customer never mentioned. VAL-0051 and VAL-0076 were Standard-plan customers told about features that the cited articles limit to Business and Enterprise plans. These examples are the basis for two changes to the requirements in Section 9.

7.7 Latency

Figure 4 shows the time taken for each ticket, and Table B4 breaks it down by outcome. The median was 6.3 seconds and the 95th percentile 55.8 seconds, which misses the 3-second target. Fifty-seven tickets finished in under 10 seconds and 21 took between 42 and 62 seconds. The run used a rate-limited free tier with request pacing and retries inside the measured time, so these are end-to-end times under those conditions and not a production figure. A fair measurement needs an unpaced run on a key with higher limits, which I did not make.

7.9 What I did not measure

I did not measure the hallucination rate or citation accuracy, because that needs two people to judge the drafts and I did not arrange it. I did not measure first contact resolution, reply time or satisfaction, since there was no pilot. I did not run an unpaced latency test, a load test or a cost per ticket. I did not run the hidden set. Groups of 8 to 61 tickets cannot show a gap smaller than about 20 points, so "no evidence of a gap" in Section 8 is a weaker statement than "no gap".
