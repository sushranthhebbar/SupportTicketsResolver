7. Evaluation

7.1 Method and data

I used three sets of tickets for different purposes. The 500 development tickets were used for analysis, for fitting calibration and for choosing between prompts and rules. The 80 validation tickets were kept for the final comparison, and I used them for no tuning. The hidden set of 120 tickets that the course will use has not been given to me, and I have run the system on it zero times; the harness takes any input path and writes the same report, so the course can run it. The validation run reported in this section used the final configuration: prompt PR-03 version 4 for classification, router version 4, and drafting prompt PR-02 version 2, with confidence calibration switched off. It processed all 80 tickets with no errors. Because of the provider's daily allowance it ran in two resumed sessions, finishing by 10 November 2026, and the report file was regenerated on 11 November to correct a latency block that had been empty.

Paraphrases of one scenario are nearly identical, so single tickets are not independent. Wherever I give an interval, it is a 95 percent interval from resampling whole scenarios (50 groups in the validation set, 76 in the development predictions), 2,000 times. A naive per-ticket interval would have been about half as wide. Where I compare two systems on the same tickets I use the paired difference.

7.2 Business outcomes

I cannot report the business outcomes the client cares about. First contact resolution, time to first reply and customer satisfaction need real tickets answered with and without the assistant, and the prototype answers none. The evaluation report therefore leaves those fields empty by design. For context, the validation tickets' own history shows what human handling achieved: first contact resolution of 46.3 percent, an escalation rate of 53.8 percent, a repeat contact rate of 21.3 percent, a mean resolution time of 441 minutes and a satisfaction average of 2.6 out of 5. These are the baseline a pilot would have to beat, not results of the system. The only business-relevant evidence I have is indirect: the system retrieves an expected article for nearly every answerable ticket, and prepares a summary for every escalation, which are the two things the interviews said would save agents' time. I did not measure the time saved.

7.4 Changing the prompt: a result that was not clean

The first classification prompt (PR-03 v2) reached 85.6 percent intent accuracy on the development predictions (interval 77.2 to 92.8). The error analysis showed that errors were concentrated: 37 of 49 errors fell in 8 of 76 scenarios, among them the compliance request "an auditor asked for access records" misread as a data export, which would have been answered instead of escalated. I wrote a fourth version of the prompt (PR-03 v4) with explicit intent definitions and tie-break rules, and, before running it, a pre-registered hypothesis, H1: that intent accuracy would improve with no class losing more than 10 points and macro recall rising. On the development tickets the result was 93.2 percent, a paired gain of 7.6 points (interval 1.6 to 14.7), with 30 tickets fixed and 4 broken. Must-escalate tickets caught rose from 56 of 64 to 64 of 64. But H1 was not met: recall for authentication failure fell by 21 points. I recorded that verdict as written and adopted the prompt anyway, because the lost class is a pair of labels that overlap and both go to a person, and the gains were larger. That was a decision, not a pass, and I list it as such in the revision log.

On validation the two prompts differ by two tickets, which I regard as no evidence either way. I used the validation set once for the final prompt and have not used it to tune anything. Because of that, I cannot test any further prompt change without a fresh, unseen set, which is a limitation of having only 80 validation tickets.

7.5 Confidence, calibration and routing

The model reports its own confidence and I hoped to turn it into a gate: answer only when calibrated confidence is above 0.80. It cannot be done with this model. PR-03 v4 emits only three distinct intent confidences (0.85, 0.95 and 0.98). On the development predictions, intent was right for 94.0 percent of the 151 tickets at 0.98 and 92.6 percent of the 188 at 0.95, a difference of 1.4 points. On validation the gap is larger (97.6 percent of 41 against 84.2 percent of 38) but rests on few tickets, and with a threshold of 0.80, below all three values, a gate would filter nothing either way. I fitted isotonic calibrations (three versions, split by scenario); the latest is flat, and none could be shown to separate right answers from wrong ones. I left calibration switched off, so with the artifact unset every ordinary ticket goes to review. That is the safest behaviour available, and it explains the routing result: the supplied labels expect 48 of the 80 validation tickets to be answered automatically and 32 to be escalated, whereas the system escalated 17 and sent 63 to an agent. Strict routing accuracy is therefore 17.5 percent (14 of 80), because the system never takes the automatic route. Of the 32 labelled for escalation, 14 were escalated and 18 went to review, where an agent sees them; none was answered automatically. I report routing accuracy and do not defend it as a success: it shows that the prototype deliberately declines to automate.

7.6 Retrieval and the drafts

Retrieval worked well. On the development tickets an expected article was among the five retrieved for 95.8 percent of answerable tickets (the first result for 88.8 percent), and on validation for 98.1 percent. Of the 63 tickets that reached drafting, 50 produced a draft with citations and 13 returned a note that evidence was insufficient; nine of those 13 were refusals because the model's text had no inline citation, a safe failure, although six of the nine concerned answerable tickets that a retry might have drafted.

Because I did not run the human review, I read the 50 drafts myself against the cited articles. I did not use the labels to judge them. The result is an informal check, not a rate: 39 drafts were sound, 7 contained a small assumption or embellishment, and 4 would mislead a reviewer who only skims (for example, a draft that told a customer to revoke a key as exposed when the ticket body described a permission-scope error, because the subject line said "exposed key"). Two drafts also described features restricted to plans the customer did not have. All 50 passed the automatic citation validation, which confirms that it checks retrieval and not whether a claim fits the ticket. The patterns, a subject line taken as fact, an article applied to a case or a plan it does not fit, and an unparsed multi-document citation, are the basis for two requirement changes in Section 9.

7.7 Latency

The median was 6.3 seconds and the 95th percentile 55.8 seconds, so the target of 3 seconds is not met. Figure 4 shows two clusters: 57 tickets under 10 seconds and 21 tickets between 42 and 62 seconds. Because the slow tickets appear in every group, including the escalations that make a single call, I infer that they waited for the provider's rate limit, but I did not log the cause, so this is an inference. The run also deliberately waited about 2.5 seconds before each model call. These are therefore end-to-end times under free-tier pacing, not a production figure, and a fair measurement needs an unpaced run on a key with higher limits, which I did not make.

7.9 What I did not measure

I did not measure the hallucination rate or citation accuracy, because that needs two people to judge the drafts and I did not do it; my own read is the only substitute and is not a rate. I did not measure first contact resolution, reply time or satisfaction, because there is no pilot. I did not run an unpaced latency test, a load test, or a cost per ticket. I did not run the hidden set. And groups of 8 to 61 tickets cannot detect a gap smaller than about 20 points, so "no evidence of a gap" in the next section is weaker than "no gap".
