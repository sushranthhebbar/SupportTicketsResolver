Section 3

Discovery findings

The evidence came from the five transcripts and from the 500 development tickets, each of which carries labels and a record of how people handled it. Every figure below comes from those tickets. The full tables are in the Stage 1 workbook.

3.1 The answers exist

357 of the 500 tickets (71.4%) are labelled answerable from the current articles. Historically, 56.2% were escalated, and 49.1% of those escalated tickets were answerable from documentation. This is the finding that moved the design away from a model that answers from its own knowledge. The system retrieves an article and cites it. In this sample first contact resolution is 43.8% and average satisfaction 2.97 out of 5, against the 42% and 3.2 that Marcus reports. The gap could come from different periods or definitions, and I treat Marcus's figures as the operating baseline.

3.2 The minutes go to a few kinds of ticket

Resolution minutes are my measure of effort. The average ticket takes 421.7 minutes. Security incidents average 785.9 minutes and make up 5.2% of tickets but 9.7% of all minutes. Feature requests average 723.9 and compliance requests 679.3. The cheap tickets are the routine ones: rate limits average 122.2 minutes, single sign-on configuration 191.0 and onboarding 202.1. Figure 1 sets each category's share of tickets against its share of minutes.

The pattern suggested a division of labour. Routine, well-documented categories are where a draft saves an agent time. Security, compliance, feature requests and unclear tickets are where a specialist is needed and where the corpus may have no answer at all, as Ines pointed out for feature requests and new incidents. I turned that into a standing rule: those four intents always escalate, whatever the model's confidence.

3.3 First contact resolution and satisfaction move together

Marcus put first contact resolution first and Ravi put waiting first. Satisfaction averages 3.41 for tickets resolved on first contact and 2.63 for the rest. It averages 3.40 for the fastest quarter of tickets and 2.63 for the slowest. The two measures overlap, since a ticket resolved first time is also a quick one, so I kept both as goals and watch satisfaction so that it does not slip while resolution improves.

3.4 The non-fluent customers

Sofia said non-fluent customers take longer and are the least satisfied. Half of that holds up. The 120 non-fluent tickets (24.0%) have a mean resolution time of 466.1 minutes against 407.7 for fluent customers. Their satisfaction, though, is 3.04 against the 2.97 average, and their first contact resolution of 45.8% is slightly above the 43.8% overall. The cost shows up as time, not as unhappiness. I treated it as a fairness risk for retrieval, which depends on a customer's wording matching the documentation, and tested it directly (Section 8).

3.5 Enterprise customers do worst

Ravi worried about a gap between business and enterprise customers. The data shows the gap, in a direction I did not expect. Enterprise tickets have the lowest first contact resolution (37.3%, against 48.2% for business and 43.1% for standard), the highest escalation rate (62.7%) and the longest mean resolution time (483.7 minutes). Any change that does not make this worse has to be checked on enterprise tickets in particular.

3.6 What nobody mentioned

Nobody raised the handling of customer information, although every ticket carries a name and an ID. That became the privacy and auditability requirements. Nobody raised differences between channels either. When I checked later, the differences I found mostly disappeared once I accounted for how near-duplicate tickets cluster (Section 7).

Problem statement, as written at the end of discovery CloudServe's support team is struggling to keep up with more than 500 tickets a week. Customers wait 8 to 12 hours for a first reply, despite the two-hour service promise, and fewer than half of tickets are resolved on first contact. Many answers already exist in the support articles, but agents struggle to find them, and escalations often arrive without clear notes on what has already been tried. Customers wait longer, and agents repeat work.
