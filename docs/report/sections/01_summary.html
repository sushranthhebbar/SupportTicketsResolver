1. Executive summary

CloudServe Solutions is a company of about 150 people whose support team of six agents receives more than 500 tickets a week. Customers wait 8 to 12 hours for a first reply against a two-hour agreement, and about 42 percent of tickets are resolved on first contact. The head of support asked for a chatbot that answers the easy tickets so that response times improve without more staff. My discovery work suggested that this was not quite the problem. In the 500 development tickets, 71.4 percent are marked answerable from the existing knowledge base, so the answers largely exist; the difficulty is that agents struggle to find them and that escalated tickets reach specialists without the context they need. A chatbot that answers on its own would also risk the outcome the head of support named as most damaging, which is a confident wrong answer.

I therefore built a support assistant for agents, not a replacement for them. For each ticket it identifies the intent and urgency, retrieves the relevant knowledge-base passages, drafts a reply that cites its sources, and, for the tickets a specialist must handle, prepares an escalation summary. An agent reads every draft before anything could reach a customer, and the system has no code path that sends a message. Every step is written to a decision log, and guardrails block tickets and drafts that contain credential-like or instruction-like text.

The results below come from the 80-ticket validation set, which I ran once in the final configuration. The run processed all 80 tickets with no errors. Intent classification was correct for 91.3 percent of tickets (73 of 80). The expected article was among the retrieved passages for 98.1 percent of answerable tickets (52 of 53). All 14 tickets that policy says must go to a specialist (security incidents, compliance requests, feature requests and unclear requests) were escalated. The decision log reconciled exactly with the tickets processed. Latency was poor: the median was 6.3 seconds and the 95th percentile 55.8 seconds, against a target of 3 seconds, although the run used a rate-limited free model tier and those figures include waiting for the provider. Urgency was predicted correctly for only 43.8 percent of tickets, no better than always answering "medium", so I made urgency advisory: it orders the agent's queue and never changes a decision.

Two further findings shaped the design. First, the model's confidence scores could not be turned into a safe gate for automatic answering: the model reports only three distinct values, and wrong answers occur at the highest of them. The system therefore sends ordinary tickets to review rather than answering them, and the "auto-respond" route means only that a draft is ready for an agent. Second, in the development labels, 67 of 97 groups of identical ticket text carry conflicting urgency labels, which limits what any urgency measurement can show.

The most important caveat is that I did not measure the hallucination rate or citation accuracy. Both need a human to judge whether each claim is supported, and I did not carry out that review. The automatic citation check proves only that a cited document was retrieved. My own informal read of 50 validation drafts found 39 sound, 7 with minor assumptions and 4 that would mislead a reviewer who only skims, and the automatic checks caught none of the four. First contact resolution, reply time and customer satisfaction cannot be measured offline, because the prototype resolves no real tickets, and the hidden 120-ticket set has not been run by me. The figures in this report therefore describe how well the assistant classifies and retrieves, and what it does when things fail; they are not evidence that it improves CloudServe's support outcomes.
